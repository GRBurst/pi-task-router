# Structural Variants for LLM Code Review in an Autonomous Merge Pipeline

For standard 150-line, 6-file branches gated by one fresh-session Claude Opus 5.5 pass where fixed prompt and context overhead constitutes 73–96% of call cost, none of the four structural variants should replace the single-pass generalist review unconditionally. Based on available literature and break-even modeling, we recommend **do not adopt** for (a) multi-diff batching, **do not adopt** for (b) multi-call role-specialized panels on standard branches (folding conformance, defect, and security checklists into the single prompt instead), **adopt with changes** for (c) interleaved checkpoints (retaining 150-line branch-level checkpointing over milestone-end deferral while adding an upstream plan-review gate and rejecting sub-branch checkpoints), and **do not adopt** for (d) adversarial failing-test generation on standard branches.  
Empirical evidence isolating these exact architectures on frontier code review remains limited. No primary study directly measures per-diff recall and precision when batching multiple independent code diffs, compares a same-tier three-role panel (conformance, defects, security) against a structured generalist prompt, quantifies checkpoint versus end-of-branch agent review costs on long-horizon milestones, or benchmarks adversarial failing-test generation head-to-head against judgement review at the same model tier. Consequently, quantitative recommendations combine indirect code-review scaling measurements—most notably a 15-fold F1 collapse in single-diff LLM code review as diffs expand past 50–150 lines1—with adjacent batch-prompting benchmarks, multi-agent ablations, and Meta's production catching-test deployment (which exhibited an 80.5% post-filter false-positive rate)[^3].

## 1. Batched Review of Independent Diffs

**Direct code-review evidence: no evidence found.** Neither peer-reviewed studies, preprints, nor production engineering reports in the retrieved literature measure per-diff defect recall or precision when multiple independent diffs are evaluated in a single LLM call—or sequentially inside one shared session—compared against reviewing each diff in a separate fresh call. What is missing is a controlled experiment varying the number of independent diffs *k* and per-diff line count *L* inside one review prompt (or cumulative session) while measuring per-diff F1, recall, precision, and positional bias.  
Evidence from non-code-review batch prompting and single-diff scaling nevertheless establishes consistent degradation mechanisms as context items multiply. [Peer-reviewed] In *Batched Self-Consistency Improves LLM Relevance Assessment and Ranking* (Anton Korikov, Pan Du, Scott Sanner, and Navid Rekabsaz, 2025, EMNLP 2025 per filename emnlp25_batchllm.pdf, https://ssanner.github.io/papers/emnlp25_batchllm.pdf), batching 10, 30, or 90 candidate passages made one-by-one pointwise ranking 15.3× slower on legal search, yet single-call batching (*m* = 1) did not beat separate evaluation (GPT-4o Legal Search NDCG@10 was 43.8% batched vs. 44.9% one-by-one); batching outperformed separate scoring by 5%–10% AUC-PR on Legal Search Shallow (and reached 51.3% vs. 46.8% NDCG@10 on GPT-4o) only when paired with *m* = 15 shuffled self-consistency calls, while reporting no quantitative lost-in-the-middle breakdown[^5]. [Vendor or practitioner blog] A secondary synthesis of batch prompting (https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/batch-prompting, citing Cheng et al. 2023 and Lin et al. 2023) notes that prefix amortization reduces input tokens from n × (P + E[|q|]) to P + n × E[|q|], but observes U-shaped positional sensitivity and accuracy loss beyond batch sizes of 4–8 unless paired with permutation ensembling[^6]. [Preprint/peer-reviewed status unconfirmed in available text] In https://openreview.net/forum?id=Agyicd577r (title and authors truncated in extract; corresponding to BatchPrompt + BPE + SEAS, Lin et al. 2023 per secondary citations[^6]), maintaining BoolQ accuracy at batch size 32 (90.6% to 90.9%, using 27.4% of baseline tokens) required Batch Permutation and Ensembling plus early stopping, consuming 15.7% of single-item LLM calls rather than a single batched pass[^8].  
Three further studies document positional and cross-item interference inside batched prompts. [Peer-reviewed] *Lost in Choice: Evaluating LLMs Under Extreme Multiple-Choice Scaling* (Association for Computational Linguistics, Findings of EMNLP 2026, Oct 24–29, 2026, pp. 6931–6963, https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-emnlp.328.pdf, Abstract and Section 3; author list not present in extract) evaluates 31,000 MMLU-XT instances and ToolBench trajectories—backed by a 200-pair audit (κ = 0.82) showing 94.0% weakest-domain precision and <4.5% global FNR—and finds accuracy collapses asymmetrically at late-middle and terminal positions rather than recovering in a U-shape[^9]. [Peer-reviewed] Another ACL Findings of EMNLP 2026 paper (https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-emnlp.119.pdf, p. 2464, Figures 2–3; title and authors not present in extract) testing batch sizes 1–128 on GPT-4.1 and GPT-4.1-mini reports that conventional single-call batch prompting underperforms unpredictably relative to single-item prompting, and even cascaded batching (peaking at 86.81% MMLU and 86.20% MNLI on GPT-4.1-mini) slightly degrades by batch size 12810. [Preprint] In BatchSafeBench (*Efficient but Vulnerable: Benchmarking and Defending LLM Batch Prompting Attack*, 2025, https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15551, Table 1 and Results; authors not listed in extract, title matched via[^6]), evaluating 8,000 batched instances showed GPT-4 series models suffer >90% average attack success rate from cross-item interference (88.8% for GPT-4o at batch size 20), and even benign-looking content appends cut accuracy by 0.5%–6% on GSM8k and 2%–13% on HotpotQA[^11]. Sequential multi-diff review inside one persistent session faces related context-bloat failure modes noted qualitatively in agent engineering posts[^12].  
For code review specifically, the closest quantitative proxy is diff-size scaling inside one prompt. [Preprint] In *Bigger Isn't Always Better: A Comparative Evaluation of LLMs for Automated Code Review* (Shivam Pankaj Kumar, Swati Bararia, and Kislay Raj, April 9, 2026, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.15689, Abstract, Section 3, and Section 4.5 / Figure 3), across 150 samples (100 synthetic mutations with median 5 and max 40 lines; 50 real GitHub PRs with median 117 and max 562 lines across 1–10 files), Claude Haiku 4.5's F1 falls from 0.657 on tiny diffs (<10 lines, n = 92) and 0.800 on small diffs (10–50 lines, n = 10) to 0.070 on medium diffs (50–150 lines, n = 34) and 0.043 on large diffs (150–600 lines, n = 14)—a 15-fold drop from small to large diffs reported in Figure 3, and a 92% collapse from synthetic diffs (F1 = 0.847) to real PRs alone (best F1 = 0.066)[^1].  
**[Inference] Break-Even Computation for Cost per Accepted-and-Correct Merge:** Let single-diff review cost for *L* changed lines be $C_1(L) = F + vL$, with fixed per-call cost $F$ and variable per-line cost $v$. At $L = 150$, the measured fixed share $s(150) = F/(F + 150v) \in [0.73, 0.96]$ (midpoint 0.845) implies $F/v = 150 \cdot s/(1 - s) \in [405.6, 3600]$ lines. Batching *k* independent diffs of length *L* into one call (ignoring output-token growth per extra finding) reduces review cost per diff to:  
$C_{\mathrm{batch}}(k, L) = \frac{F}{k} + vL = C_1(L)\left[\frac{s(L)}{k} + (1 - s(L))\right], \quad s(L) = \frac{F/v}{F/v + L}$  
On pure review spend per caught defect ($C_{\mathrm{batch}}/R_k \leq C_1/R_1$, holding false positives constant), batching breaks even only if relative recall loss $\delta_k = 1 - R_k/R_1$ stays below $\delta_{\mathrm{max,review}}(k, L) = s(L)(1 - 1/k)$. However, an autonomous merge pipeline pays downstream escape cost $D_{\mathrm{esc}}$ on missed defects occurring at branch defect rate $p_d$, giving total cost per branch $C_{\mathrm{total}}(k, L) = C_{\mathrm{batch}}(k, L) + p_d(1 - R_k)D_{\mathrm{esc}}$ (excluding extra local rework from false rejections). Batching reduces total cost per accepted-and-correct merge only if fixed-cost savings outweigh added defect escapes:  
$F\left(1 - \frac{1}{k}\right) > p_d R_1 \delta_k D_{\mathrm{esc}} \iff \delta_k < \frac{F(1 - 1/k)}{p_d R_1 D_{\mathrm{esc}}}$

**[Inference]** Under cited parameters, batching loses across standard 150-line branches and remains marginal even on small diffs. First, on 150-line diffs, batching just *k* = 2 puts 300 changed lines in context; extrapolating from Kumar et al.'s single-diff drop from Medium (50–150 lines, F1 = 0.070) to Large (150–600 lines, F1 = 0.043) yields a **38.6% relative F1 loss**[^1], already exceeding the 36.5% lower-bound savings at *s* = 0.73 before counting escape penalties (though independent diffs avoid cross-file coupling and may lose less than single PRs). Second, escape-cost asymmetry shrinks tolerable recall loss to near zero: [Vendor or practitioner blog] newmarketpitch.com (2026, https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/ai-code-assistant-agents-too-expensive) reports Claude Opus 5.5 list prices around \$4/M input and \$20/M output (and self-hosted PR-Agent reviews run \$0.02–\$0.25 per baeseokjae.github.io[^14]), placing *F* around ~\$0.05–\$0.30, whereas senior debugging and rework of an escaped AI patch is estimated at \$300–\$450 (2–3 hrs at \$150/hr)or~\$500 (3.3 hrs)[^15]. Even with baseline recall $R_1 \approx 0.25$[^1] and 20% defect rate ($p_d R_1 = 0.05$), $F = \$0.10$ and $D_{\mathrm{esc}} = \$300$ yields $p_d R_1 D_{\mathrm{esc}}/F = 150$, permitting at most **0.33%–0.58%** relative recall loss across *k* = 2–8; even if automated local rework catches escaped bugs for only \$20 ($p_d R_1 D_{\mathrm{esc}}/F = 10$), tolerable recall loss is **5.0%–7.5%**, below the 0.5%–13% interference drops observed in BatchSafeBench[^11]. The narrowest theoretical window where batching could help is *k* = 2 tiny diffs of 10–25 lines each (staying within the 10–50 line sweet spot where Kumar et al. measured F1 = 0.800)[^1].

## 2. Role-Specialized Single-Pass Panels

**Evidence by objective (same-tier dedicated vs. generalist):** Evaluating the three requested objectives reveals unequal coverage across the literature. For **plan/goal conformance**, **no evidence found** comparing a dedicated conformance-review prompt against a generalist diff prompt at the same model tier with F1, recall, or precision; [Preprint] https://arxiv.org/html/2609.01087v1 (title and authors not present in extract; 2026 per ID) shows supervised fine-tuning of GPT-4o, CodeLlama-7B, and CodeQwen1.5 improves PR–issue alignment classification qualitatively, without reporting benchmark metrics against a generalist review[^16]. For **defect detection**, **no direct same-tier generalist vs. single-objective defect prompt benchmark was found**; in [Preprint] CodeX-Verify (https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16708v1, Nov 2025, Section 6.2; Shreshth Rajan per official repo https://github.com/ShreshthRajan/codex-verify, full paper title absent from extract), four specialized agents (Correctness, Security, Performance, Style) operating without code execution at sub-200 ms latency averaged 32.8% accuracy individually (ranging 17.2%–75.9% across 99 labeled SWE-bench failure samples in 16 categories) against a 40% Codex baseline, without isolating Correctness F1 against a generalist prompt[^17]. Indirectly, Table 6 of Kumar et al. (arXiv:2606.15689, n = 1501) confirms a single generalist prompt struggles heavily on non-security defects, capturing 12.7%–24.5% of logic bugs (Haiku 4.5 24.5%, Sonnet 4.6 19.6%), 13.6%–33.3% of architecture bugs, 0.0%–6.7% of best-practice issues, and 0.0%–4.5% of performance bugs.  
For **security**, dedicated structured workflows measurably beat generic same-tier baselines. [Preprint] In VIC-RAGENT (https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05772v1, July 2026 per ID, Table 3 and ablation; title and authors not listed in extract), prompting the same underlying LLM to check commits against 25 predefined vulnerability factors lifted F1 on DeepSeek-V3.2 to **57% vs. 33%** for CodeAgent, whereas Chain-of-Thought achieved 86% precision at only 6% recall (and Direct prompting lagged VIC-RAGENT across DeepSeek-V3.2, GPT-4o-mini, and Qwen-Plus); dropping the Stage 3 final verification step raised false positives from 22 to 30 and cut true negatives from 113 to 10519. [Preprint, cited via arXiv:2511.16708] Yujia Chen's *AutoReview: An LLM-based Multi-Agent System for Security Issue-Oriented Code Review* (July 28, 2025, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394078105_AutoReview_An_LLM-based_Multi-Agent_System_for_Security_Issue-Oriented_Code_Review) increased security F1 by **+18.72%** on ReposVul[^20]. [Preprint/unverified listing] A separate seven-agent CLI system, *A Multi-Agent System for Automated Pre-Push Code Review* (AutoReview CLI, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/397984834_A_Multi-Agent_System_for_Automated_Pre-Push_Code_Review, Section 5.1–5.2 and Tables 1–2; authors and date not shown in extract), evaluated 200 changesets across 5 projects and reported **78% recall** vs. **55%** for manual review in 58 seconds (\$0.34/review), with its Security Agent finding **91%** of ground-truth security vulnerabilities (+87% critical security detection, p < 0.001), Security + Quality + Testing capturing 73% of critical findings, and Performance + Architecture finding 94% of performance-critical issues despite contributing 18% of findings—yet it did not benchmark a single generalist LLM prompt[^22]. Meanwhile, generalist prompts in Kumar et al. (arXiv:2606.15689, Table 6[^1]) already attained **69.6%–71.1%** security recall across five models.  
Sources diverge sharply on when combining multiple reviewers beats a single reviewer, depending on error independence and verification gating. When specialized agents exhibit low pairwise error correlation (ρ = 0.05–0.25 in CodeX-Verify Table 3), combining them yields diminishing yet substantial accuracy gains: 32.8% (1-agent avg) → 47.7% (+14.9 pp, 2 agents) → 61.2% (+13.5 pp, 3 agents) → 72.4% (+11.2 pp, 4 agents), with [Secondary synthesis] https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/codex-verify reporting the best 2-agent pair (Correctness + Performance) at 79.3% accuracy (83.3% TPR, 40.0% FPR) vs. 75.9% for the strongest single agent[^17]. Crucially, naive union in CodeX-Verify yielded an **80% FPR** (75% TPR); calibrated severity weighting (0.45, 0.35, 0.15, 0.05 vs. uniform 0.25) improved F1 from 0.65 to 0.78, yet final FPR remained **50.0%** at 76.1% TPR (68.7% ± 9.1% accuracy)[^17]. Similarly, [Preprint] *Adversarial Review: Structured Disagreement for Grounded Agentic Code Review* (Cornell and Stanford, https://openreview.net/pdf?id=fOHvpLs6zp, Table 1 and Section 4.5; full author list and date truncated in extract) found that pairing a reviewer with an evidence-enforcing critic (3 agents) achieved **87%** pass rate on LiveCodeBench (75% on hard tasks) and **F1 = 0.533** on diff review—whereas an ungrounded "Two-reviewers" panel (3 agents) *dropped* pass rate from 77% (Zero-shot / Single-reviewer) to **75%** (60% on hard tasks) due to sycophantic false agreement[^24]. Likewise, Kumar et al. (arXiv:2606.15689, Section 4.7 / Table 9[^1]) reported that pairing two different models on the same generalist prompt lowered F1 from **0.365** (Haiku 4.5 alone: P = 0.486, R = 0.293) to **0.333** (Haiku ∪ Sonnet 4.6: P = 0.627, R = 0.226) and **0.304** (Haiku ∪ GLM-5 Turbo: P = 0.694, R = 0.195), with 55/150 samples (36.7%) flagged by all five models and 19/150 (12.7%) missed by all—though the simultaneous rise in precision and drop in recall under ∪ suggests an undocumented consensus/filtering step in Table 9 rather than raw set union. [Peer-reviewed] Finally, on deterministic probe-bounded crypto dependency transitions (CryptoUpdate-Mini-30), MDPI *Computers* 2026, 15(9), 638 (https://www.mdpi.com/2073-431X/15/9/638, Section 4.1 / Table 5; title and authors not in extract) found that single-agent and Planner–Verifier matched the full multi-agent VeriCrypt-Agent on transition macro-F1 (though routing 24/30 vs. 9/30 runs to review), while an exhaustive deterministic probe gate outperformed all LLM variants (10/10 correct transitions)[^25].  
Commercial benchmarks and engineering posts reinforce the role of a verification pass to control false positives. [Vendor or practitioner blog] Bae Seokjae (https://baeseokjae.github.io/posts/qodo-review-2026/, 2026) cites independent benchmarks showing Qodo 2.0's February 2026 multi-agent architecture at **60.1% F1** (under its ~800-line truncation limit) vs. ~54% bug catch rate for GitHub Copilot[^14], while [Vendor or practitioner blog] MorphLLM (https://www.morphllm.com/comparisons/greptile-vs-copilot, March 14, 2026) reports Greptile at **50.2% F1** (66.2% precision, 40.4% recall) vs. Copilot at **44.5% F1** (56.5% precision, 36.7% recall) on separate leaderboard evaluations[^26]. [Vendor or practitioner blog] G-Research (https://www.gresearch.com/news/building-a-code-review-tool-the-llm-patterns-that-actually-work/) found single-pass review produced ~8 findings with 2–3 false positives and solved this by splitting recall generation and precision filtering into two sequential passes ("two simple passes outperform one complex prompt"), achieving >85% precision thresholds in integration tests[^27].  
**Duplicate-finding rate, cost multiplier, and conformance model downgrade:** **No evidence found** measuring exact duplicate-finding rates across conformance, defect, and security passes (the closest proxy is Kumar et al.'s 36.7% five-model overlap[^1], and skmdroid/quorum[^28] plus Ashishkosana/review-lens[^29] note that deduplicating synthesizers or adversarial verify steps are required). **No evidence found** directly testing whether a conformance-only pass can be downgraded to a cheaper model tier. Indirect model-tier results conflict: without tool grounding, Haiku 4.5 (\$0.003/review)beatSonnet4.6(\$0.010/review) on recall (0.293 vs. 0.248, +18.1%) and F1 (0.365 vs. 0.343) in Kumar et al. (arXiv:2606.15689, Table 5[^1]), while Sonnet 4.6 retained higher precision (0.558 vs. 0.486) and severity-weighted F1 (0.543 vs. 0.485); conversely, when grounded in Ruff, ESLint, Semgrep, and Bandit outputs ([Preprint] https://www.researchgate.net/publication/416504011_A_Multi-Agent_Framework_for_Evidence-Grounded_Automated_Code_Review, Section 3[^30]), GPT-4o-mini nearly matched GPT-4-Turbo-preview (97.83% precision / 52.94% recall vs. 100% / 55.56% at 33.0 s avg), whereas Claude-3-Haiku-20241022 dropped to 96.15% actionability / 47.22% recall. **[Inference]** Under fixed-cost-dominated billing, running three separate single-pass reviews at Opus 5.5 tier costs **3.0×** baseline (plus synthesis overhead); downgrading the conformance pass to Sonnet 5.5 (\$2/\$10 vs. Opus 5.5 \$4/\$20) or DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (\$0.30/\$1.20) per newmarketpitch.com list prices[^15] cuts total review cost to **2.50×** or **~2.07×**, respectively.

## 3. Interleaved Per-Task Checkpoints vs. End-of-Branch Review

**Controlled empirical comparison: no evidence found.** No study in the retrieved literature directly compares per-checkpoint LLM code review against end-of-branch LLM review on identical multi-task coding-agent workloads with measurements of drift rate, accumulated-defect detection, or cost per accepted-and-correct milestone.  
Indirect quantitative measurements point consistently toward smaller, checkpointed diffs and independent reviewer sessions. First, Kumar et al.'s diff-size curve (arXiv:2606.15689, Section 4.5 / Figure 3[^1]) shows F1 collapsing from **0.800** (10–50 lines) and **0.657** (<10 lines) to **0.070** (50–150 lines) and **0.043** (150–600 lines) due to formatting noise and multi-file interactions[^1]. **[Inference]** Deferring two 150-line branches into one 300-line milestone-end review pushes the diff squarely into the 150–600 line failure regime (an inferred 38.6% drop relative to 50–150 lines) while allowing step-2 edits to compound defects introduced in step 1. Second, [Preprint] an empirical analysis of 278,790 inline review conversations across 300 GitHub projects (https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15911v2, March 2026 per ID, RQ2 and Fig. 5; title and authors not in extract) found human reviews of AI-generated code require **11.8% more back-and-forth exchange rounds** than human code, while >95% of AI reviewer comments target Code Improvement and Defect Detection (Cursor 99% defect detection vs. Claude Code 20% vs. 40% agent average)[^31]. [Vendor or practitioner blog] CodeRabbit's analysis of 470 PRs (https://www.coderabbit.ai/guides/coding-agent-workflow) similarly reports AI co-authored PRs averaged **10.83 issues vs. 6.45** for human-only PRs[^32]. Third, [Preprint summary via Hugging Face Papers] (https://huggingface.co/papers?q=review%20agent, full citation truncated in search listing) reports LLMs fail to disclose synthetic mistakes prefilled into trajectories in **36.4%** of chat rollouts and **67.1%** of agentic rollouts, reinforcing practitioner evidence (https://medium.com/@pvginkel/my-ai-workflow-part-7-running-slices-with-pre-flight-and-verification-4d5812d42c90) that checkpoint reviews must run in a fresh reviewer session rather than inside the generator's context[^33].  
On **fix-cost asymmetry**, **no peer-reviewed empirical measurements were found** comparing defects caught at an agent checkpoint versus at milestone completion. Only unrefereed practitioner and industry estimates exist: [Vendor or practitioner blog] a post on dev.to (https://dev.to/gde/giving-ai-pain-the-architecture-of-synthetic-scars-the-rapid-regret-miner-1740) asserts across 238 production tickets (without presenting an audited cost ledger in the extract) that Boehm's Cost-of-Defect curve translates to **\$1 : \$500 : \$25,000** across stages[^35], while newmarketpitch.com estimates that a few dollars of coding-agent tokens can trigger \$300–\$**450** (2–3 hours) to **~\$500** (3.3 hours) of senior engineering rework at \$150/hour[^15].  
**[Inference] Cost Multiplier across Checkpoint Granularities:** Retaining the pipeline's current **branch-level checkpoints** (two separate 150-line reviews vs. one 300-line milestone-end review) incurs a review-call multiplier of $2/(2 - s(150)) =$ **1.57×** (at *s* = 0.73) to **1.92×** (at *s* = 0.96), or **1.95× to 2.78×** for three 150-line branches vs. one 450-line diff. By contrast, adding *intra-branch* sub-checkpoints—such as pausing for three 50-line Opus 5.5 reviews inside one 150-line branch—multiplies review cost by $1 + 2s(150) =$ **2.46× to 2.92×** while stalling the single local Qwen 3.8 GPU inference slot between edits.  
For **plan-review-then-diff-review pipelines**, architectural reports define clear stage responsibilities, though **no evidence found** reporting empirical defect-detection shares (recall/F1) broken out by stage:

| Pipeline Stage | Defect Classes Targeted | Sources & Epistemic Status |

## 4. Adversarial and Test-Generation Reviewers

**Head-to-head yield vs. judgement review and cost per true defect: no evidence found.** No study benchmarks adversarial failing-test generation against single-pass judgement review at the same model tier on the same PR corpus, nor reports dollar or token cost per true defect found. However, primary peer-reviewed and preprint measurements quantify diff-level feasibility, catching-test yield, and oracle failure modes.  
[Peer-reviewed] Meta's *Just-in-Time Catching Test Generation at Meta* (Matthew Becker et al., 15 authors, arXiv Jan 30, 2026 / *Proceedings of the 34th ACM International Conference on the Foundations of Software Engineering (FSE Companion '26)*, June 2026, pp. 282–293, https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22832 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.22832, Abstract and Sections 1–3) reports the largest industrial evaluation found, operating directly on **diffs** across backend systems of hundreds of millions of lines of code[^3]. Rather than scanning entire repositories, Meta deployed two diff-aware workflows—generating tests on the parent revision from inferred diff intent, and using Meta's ACH generator to treat the diff itself as a mutant to kill—which required parent and child build/test execution[^4]. Across **22,126 generated tests**, diff-aware workflows produced **~4×** as many weak catches (tests passing on parent, failing on diff) as traditional coverage-hardening workflows and **~20×** as many as a coincidental-failure baseline[^3]. Because weak catches mostly reflect intended behavior changes, Meta manually studied **600 diffs with weak catches**, finding an LLM-as-judge ensemble (supplemented by rule-based RubFake in production) cut human review load by **~70%** (~4× capacity scaling)[^3]. Even *after* that 70% filter, out of **41 engineer reach-outs**, only **8 were immediately confirmed as strong catches**—an **engineer-experienced true positive rate of 19.5%** (meaning **80.5% false positives** among filtered alerts), with **4 (9.8% of reach-outs)** averting serious production failures[^3]. Dismissing false positives took engineers only a few minutes, though the authors explicitly emphasize that catching test generation is "inherently computationally expensive" because many tests must be generated per diff[^3]. [Vendor or practitioner blog] Meta's October 29, 2026 engineering post (https://engineering.fb.com/2026/10/29/security/catching-bugs-before-they-land-jittestings-human-on-the-loop-approach-at-full-scale/) details the production rollout[^46]; secondary summaries of that post (https://checkops.com/blog/meta-jittests-just-in-time-tests and https://en.mundoia.com.ar/meta-ia-pruebas-errores-privacidad-jittests/) report **74 serious privacy bugs** caught at the diff stage in the first half of 202647. **[Inference]** Dividing Meta's 8 confirmed catches by the implied ~136.7 pre-filter candidates (41 / 0.30 surviving a ~70% judge reduction) suggests a raw pre-filter strong-catch yield near **~5.9%**.  
Two major technical obstacles explain low standalone yields in LLM-generated catching tests. First, [Preprint] an empirical study across **145 PRs from SciPy, Qiskit, and pandas** (https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11835v1, Oct 2026 per ID, Abstract and Answer to RQ1; title and authors not in extract) found only **2.4%–4.8%** of LLM-generated tests were fault-revealing, whereas **8.4%–16.9%** (8%–17% in Abstract)—**3.5–6.5× higher**—were **fault-enforcing**, encoding buggy diff behavior as expected truth (with 99.8% of comparable pairs asserting behavior introduced in the PR); 83%–91% persisted longitudinally and 83%–92% remained enforced when faults accumulated while existing suites caught only 14%–30%[^49]. Only 5.2% (249 of 4,763) of invalid tests failed at import or collection; the vast majority executed and failed on both versions[^49]. Second, [Preprint] a failure taxonomy comparing **263 AssertFlip** and **464 OpenHands** bug-reproduction tests (https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13395v1, Nov 2026 per ID, RQ2, RQ3, and Table II; title and authors not in extract) recorded high mechanical and environment failure rates on GPT-4o-mini (OpenHands: **64.8% Mechanical Failure**, including **41.3% Environment Error**, plus **25.0% Misimplementations**; AssertFlip: **14.1% Mechanical Failure**, 0.0% Environment Error, **25.4% Misimplementations**), dropping on GPT-5-mini to **21.8% Mechanical Failure** and **15.1% Misimplementations** on OpenHands; issue augmentation resolved 7 of 11 previously failing cases[^50]. Consistently, [Preprint/thesis] a University of Waterloo study (https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/bitstreams/481ed117-08da-4c16-815b-23ea121afcb6/download, Section 2.2 and Results; author and date not in extract) measured open-source test accuracy on correct code alone at 22.93% (Llama-3.3-70B), 18.01% (Codestral-22B), and 11.94% (Llama-3.1-8B), confirming that pairing explicit requirements with code increases bug detection[^51]. [Secondary summary] On PR review quality, PyPI's jittest summary (https://pypi.org/project/jittest/) cites BSG-VA (arXiv:2607.28871, Xu & Wu, 2026), where differential test execution shifted evidence-inadequate PR closure by +7.8 pp, below its 10 pp pre-registered threshold (~1/3 attributable to reminder prompts)[^52].  
Compared qualitatively to judgement-style review—which reaches 48.6%–55.8% precision on mixed synthetic/real diffs (arXiv:2606.15689, albeit 0.066 real-PR F1)[^1], 56.5%–66.2% precision in commercial tools (morphllm.com)[^26], and 96.15%–100% precision with static-tool grounding (researchgate.net/publication/416504011)[^30]—adversarial test generation has far lower raw precision (19.5% even post-LLM-judge) and misses non-executable architectural or style issues, yet delivers high-severity, concrete reproduction evidence when a true fault is hit[^3].

## 6. Concrete Recommendations for the Standard-Branch Gate

All quantitative projections below carry an explicit **[Inference — Extrapolated]** flag, because no cited primary source evaluates Claude Opus 5.5, Qwen 3.8 27B, or this exact 150-line / 6-file deterministic gate.

### Variant (a) Batched Review: Do Not Adopt

Do not batch multiple 150-line standard branches into one Opus 5.5 call or shared review session. [Inference — Extrapolated] Amortizing fixed cost *F* (*s* = 0.73–0.96) across *k* = 2–4 diffs cuts per-branch review API spend by **36.5%–72.0%**, but expands the reviewed diff to 300–600 lines. Extrapolating from arXiv:2606.15689's Haiku 4.5 single-diff buckets (0.070 F1 at 50–150 lines to 0.043 at 150–600 lines) implies up to a **38.6% F1 penalty**[^1], alongside cross-item accuracy interference (0.5%–13% in BatchSafeBench[^11]) and terminal/middle positional decay[^6]. Because downstream rework or bad-merge recovery ($D_{\mathrm{esc}}$, estimated at \$300–\$500 in newmarketpitch.com for senior review[^15]) exceeds sub-dollar per-call savings by one to three orders of magnitude, even a **0.3%–7.5% relative recall loss** raises cost per accepted-and-correct merge while serializing branches ahead of Qwen 3.8's single GPU slot.

### Variant (b) Role-Specialized Single-Pass Panels: Do Not Adopt

Do not split standard-branch Opus 5.5 reviews into three separate calls for plan conformance, defects, and security. [Inference — Extrapolated] Under fixed-cost dominance, three same-tier calls triple review spend to **3.0×** baseline (or **2.50×** with conformance on Sonnet 5.5 and **~2.07×** on DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, extrapolated from newmarketpitch.com list prices[^15]), before counting deduplication overhead. Because no evidence demonstrates that three separate calls outperform one structured generalist frontier prompt containing dedicated conformance, defect, and security sections—and multi-agent aggregation without filtering caused 50%–80% false-positive rates in CodeX-Verify (arXiv:2511.16708v1[^17])—expected cost per accepted-and-correct merge worsens by **+107% to +200%** for unvalidated recall gains. Keep a **1.0×** structured multi-lens single prompt on standard branches, and reserve a second precision-filtering pass (per G-Research[^27] and VIC-RAGENT[^19]) or specialist split strictly for **elevated** branches.

### Variant (c) Interleaved Checkpoints vs. End-of-Branch Review: Adopt with Changes

Keep **branch-level checkpointing** (~150 lines) rather than deferring review to end-of-milestone, **add** one fresh Opus 5.5 **Plan-Review** call upstream of Qwen 3.8 execution for multi-branch milestones, and **do not adopt** intra-branch sub-checkpoints inside a 150-line branch. [Inference — Extrapolated] Retaining per-branch review over a 300-line milestone-end diff costs **1.57×–1.92×** in review calls (extrapolated from *s* ∈ [0.73, 0.96]), yet avoids the >150-line F1 = 0.043 cliff (arXiv:2606.156891) and prevents downstream branches from amplifying flawed interfaces. Upstream plan review targets architectural, contract, and failure-mode defects (arXiv:2605.22794v1[^36]) before consuming local GPU inference, lowering total rework cost per milestone. Conversely, intra-branch 50-line checkpoints inflate fixed-cost review spend to **2.46×–2.92×** and stall the single GPU slot; when extra recall is needed, prefer decomposing work into smaller ~50-line independent branches (toward the 10–50 line F1 = 0.800 sweet spot[^1]).

### Variant (d) Adversarial / Test-Generation Reviewers: Do Not Adopt

Do not deploy failing-test generation on standard branches. [Inference — Extrapolated] Meta's Catching JiTTests (arXiv:2601.228323) shows diff-aware catching tests prevent high-severity outages (4 of 8 confirmed catches; 74 privacy bugs reported in H1 2026 via secondary summaries[^47]), but only after multi-sample test generation, parent/child test execution, and a 70% LLM-judge filter—which still left an **80.5% false-positive rate** (19.5% TPR), while arXiv:2610.11835v1 found **8.4%–16.9%** of generated tests became fault-enforcing[^49]. On standard 150-line branches, false test failures would trigger costly local Qwen 3.8 rework loops and sharply increase cost per accepted-and-correct merge. If adopted later, confine adversarial catching-test generation to **elevated** branches, ground prompts in explicit acceptance criteria (uwspace.uwaterloo.ca[^51]), filter outputs with an LLM-judge, and discard failing tests after verification rather than committing them[^47].

## 7. Least-Certain Claims

> 1. **Extrapolating arXiv:2606.15689 single-PR diff scaling to batched independent diffs and Claude Opus 5.5:** The 38.6% F1 drop from 50–150 lines (0.070) to 150–600 lines (0.043) and 0.800 peak at 10–50 lines (n = 10) were measured on Claude Haiku 4.5 over single multi-file PRs, not on Opus 5.5 reviewing concatenated independent diffs[^1].  
> 2. **Table 9 ensemble behavior in arXiv:2606.15689:** Table 9 labels model pairings with union (∪) while precision rises (0.486 to 0.627–0.694) and recall drops (0.293 to 0.195–0.226), contradicting raw set union and indicating an unstated intersection or filtering step[^1].  
> 3. **Unbenchmarked standard-branch panel, conformance-downgrade, and checkpoint cost projections:** Expected multipliers for 3-role panels (2.07×–3.0×), milestone-end savings vs. branch checkpoints (1.57×–1.92×), and conformance tier downgrades derive from fixed-cost algebra and newmarketpitch.com list prices[^15] rather than empirical trials, with no direct benchmark of duplicate-finding rates or same-tier structured generalist prompts.  
> 4. **Practitioner defect-escape rework ratios:** The \$1 : \$500 : \$25,000 Boehm translation (dev.to/gde[^35]) and \$300–\$500 senior debugging rework figures (newmarketpitch.com[^15]) come from unrefereed practitioner/industry estimates without independently audited financial ledgers.  
> 5. **Secondary and unattributed commercial/benchmark figures:** Commercial tool comparisons (Greptile 50.2% vs. Copilot 44.5% F1 on morphllm.com[^26]; Qodo 2.0 60.1% F1 vs. Copilot ~54% bug catch on baeseokjae.github.io[^14]), Meta's H1 2026 figure of 74 privacy bugs (checkops.com[^47], mundoia[^48]), and our inferred ~5.9% pre-filter strong-catch yield for Meta JiTTests[^3] rely on vendor posts, secondary summaries, or back-calculation across non-identical test beds.
