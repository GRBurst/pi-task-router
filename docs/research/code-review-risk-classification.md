# Classifying Code Changes for Token-Gated LLM Merge Review

A local git merge gate that decides whether to spend LLM tokens before invoking a model should implement a deterministic, three-tier classifier (Elevated, Standard, and Inert) built on change size, diff diffusion, and strict path-and-AST exclusions rather than a trained machine learning defect predictor [inference]. Empirical defect-prediction benchmarks show that simple change-size heuristics capture nearly all transferable risk signal without maintaining a training pipeline: on an extended 310,000-commit dataset, LApredict—a single-feature logistic regression on added line count (LA)—outperformed deep neural models (DeepJIT and CC2Vec) in defect prediction while running 81,000× faster in training and 120,000× faster in testing than CC2Vec (Deep just-in-time defect prediction: how far are we?, Zhengran Zeng, Yuqun Zhang, Lingming Zhang, et al., ISSTA 2021, July 11–17, 2021, https://zhangyuqun.github.io/publications/issta2021.pdf, Abstract, Section 1, and Section 4; exact LApredict AUC, F1, Recall@20%LOC, and Popt figures unavailable in the retrieved text) [peer-reviewed][^1]. Production AI review bots (CodeRabbit, Greptile) and LLM-assisted security triage pipelines (AquilaX, SAST-Genius) likewise rely on deterministic file-path globs, metadata filters, and upstream static analysis before invoking frontier LLMs, whereas SAST/SCA platforms (Snyk, SonarQube) use deterministic risk ranking and diff-scoping to control alert volume [vendor or practitioner blog][^4].  
Two empirical cautions govern which changes qualify for the Inert (zero-token skip-review) tier [inference]. First, configuration files must not be treated as inert: an empirical systems survey reports that parameter misconfigurations account for 70.0%–85.5% of configuration errors, 27%–51% of configuration parameters carry hidden cross-parameter correlations, and 21.7%–57.3% span multiple components or hosts (Systems Approaches to Tackling Configuration Errors: A Survey, *ACM Computing Surveys*, Vol. 47, No. 4, Article 70, July 2015, https://tianyin.github.io/pub/csur.pdf, Section 2 and Section 2.4, page 70:7, authors unnamed in retrieved text, citing Yin et al., SOSP '11, and Zhang et al., 2014) [peer-reviewed][^12]. Second, high test coverage on modified production code does not justify skipping review: an open-source study co-authored by Nachiappan Nagappan of Microsoft Research found no statistically significant correlation between code coverage and post-release bugs per line of code (IEEE Transactions on Reliability, DOI 10.1109/TR.2017.2727062, title and lead authors unavailable in retrieved text, https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=4840\&context=sis_research, Abstract and RQ1) [peer-reviewed][^13]. Furthermore, no evidence was found in peer-reviewed literature directly measuring either LLM review defect yield across change classes or the exact financial cost ratio between skipping a risky PR and reviewing an inert one [inference].

## 1. Existing Triage Practices Across Industry, Bots, and Standards

### Industrial Pull Request Risk Prediction

Published industrial engineering work favors uniform lightweight review gates integrated with automated static checks over ML-driven skip-review gating [peer-reviewed][^14]. At Google, virtually every change to its monolithic repository passes through the internal web tool Critique, which displays static analysis findings and reviewer recommendations alongside the diff (Modern Code Review: A Case Study at Google, Google, ICSE-SEIP '18, May 27–June 3, 2018, https://sback.it/publications/icse2018seip.pdf, Section 3.2 and Section 5.1; authors unnamed in retrieved text) [peer-reviewed][^15]. Analyzing 9 million reviewed changes, 12 interviews, and 44 survey responses (https://research.google/pubs/modern-code-review-a-case-study-at-google/, Abstract), the Google study reports a median review latency under 4 hours across all change sizes—far shorter than Rigby and Bird's benchmarks of 17.5 hours for AMD, 15.7 hours for Chrome OS, and 14.7, 19.8, and 18.9 hours across three Microsoft projects—and finds that even very large changes average fewer than two reviewers, with one reviewer often deemed sufficient (https://sback.it/publications/icse2018seip.pdf, Section 5.2, Finding 4) [peer-reviewed][^14]. Google developers author a median of roughly 3 changes per week (80% below 7), review a median of 4 per week (80% below 10), and spend an average of 3.2 hours per week reviewing (median 2.6 hours, versus 6.4 hours/week self-reported in open-source projects) (https://sback.it/publications/icse2018seip.pdf, Section 5.2 and Section 6) [peer-reviewed][^16].  
At Microsoft, published research centers on risk prediction for testing and inspection allocation rather than skipping human review [inference]. Zimmermann and Nagappan modeled defect proneness from Windows Server 2003 binary dependency graphs (Predicting Defects using Network Analysis on Dependency Graphs, *Proceedings of ICSE '08*, May 2008, pp. 531–540, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/predicting-defects-using-network-analysis-on-dependency-graphs/ and https://www.scilit.com/publications/fafc5a2b6b1ec012d2d1765fd8da2c78) [peer-reviewed][^17], while subsequent Microsoft Research work showed that pre-release test failure bursts and failure scenario diversity outperform raw pre-release defect counts in predicting post-release bugs (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/Using-Pre-Release-Test-Failures-to-Build-Early-Post-Release-Defect-Prediction-Models.pdf, 2014/11/ upload, title and authors unnamed in retrieved text) [peer-reviewed][^19]. In a Microsoft practitioner study (165 initial survey responses at a 28% response rate, followed by a 2,000-person survey with 873 responses on awareness items), programmers ranked "finding defects" as their top motivation (383 programmers, or 44%, first; 204, or 23%, second; 96, or 11%, third), ahead of "code improvement" (337, or 39%, first; 208, or 24%, second; 135, or 15%, third), "team awareness" (75, or 9%, first), and "knowledge transfer" (73, or 8%, first), yet defect comments ranked only fourth among nine comment categories at 78 comments (14%) (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/ICSE202013-codereview.pdf, ICSE202013 filename, uploaded February 2016, title and authors unnamed in retrieved text) [peer-reviewed][^20].  
No evidence was found of published internal pull-request risk-routing or token-gating architectures from GitHub, GitLab, or Meta; accessible references to GitHub and GitLab cover only status-check APIs (https://docs.coderabbit.ai/reference/configuration) [vendor or practitioner blog][^4], while a panel summary on Moderne's blog covering Google, Meta, DX, and Moderne discusses organizational outcomes of AI-assisted maintenance—reporting DX survey findings across more than 135,000 developers in 425 organizations that AI halves customer-facing incidents on some teams while doubling them on others depending on testing, review, and SRE maturity, alongside a 30% single-quarter feature delivery gain at an insurance firm and higher merge rates when teams self-trigger automated PRs (https://moderne.ai/blog/ai-driven-code-maintenance-what-works-at-meta-google-and-other-large-enterprises, undated) [vendor or practitioner blog][^21].

In SAST prioritization and LLM-assisted triage, pipelines consistently combine cheap upstream deterministic rules with downstream contextual LLM verification [vendor or practitioner blog][^10]. In SAST-Genius (Hybrid SAST + LLM Code Security, Virelya Research, undated, authors unnamed, https://www.virelya.org/research/hybrid-sast-llm-code-security, Evaluation and Table 1), a triage script extracts cross-file function definitions and taint flows from Semgrep findings before prompting an LLM and generating exploit PoCs [vendor or practitioner blog][^10]. Across a ground-truth benchmark of 170 vulnerabilities, SAST-Genius reported 89.5% precision, 100% recall, and 94.5% F1—compared with 35.7% precision / 73.5% recall for Semgrep alone and 65.5% precision / 77.1% recall for GPT-4 alone—while producing valid PoCs for approximately 70% of exploitable findings and introducing no false positives outside the union of Semgrep and GPT-4 (https://www.virelya.org/research/hybrid-sast-llm-code-security) [vendor or practitioner blog][^10]. Similarly, Datadog engineers report that LLM false-positive filtering relies directly on underlying SAST rule quality and faces an explicit prompt-tuning trade-off between catching true positives and suppressing false positives (Using LLMs to filter out false positives from static code analysis, Cole Maring and Kassen Qian, Datadog blog, undated, https://www.datadoghq.com/blog/using-llms-to-filter-out-false-positives/) [vendor or practitioner blog][^42], while a January 2026 preprint notes that triage agents often adopt worst-case threat models that inflate recall while eroding false-positive precision (https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22952v2, arXiv:2601.22952v2) [preprint][^43].  
For tiered model routing, AquilaX outlines a three-stage SAST escalation architecture (Using AI to Triage SAST False Positives Without Burning Out Your Team, AquilaX DevSecOps Team, March 2026, https://aquilax.ai/blog/ai-sast-false-positive-triage, Step 3 and "Avoiding Blind Spots") [vendor or practitioner blog][^11]: deterministic rule-based filters (suppressing test files and known-safe patterns) first, a fine-tuned compact model (CodeBERT or StarCoder 1B trained on ~500 labeled examples per vulnerability class) second, and frontier LLM reasoning (GPT-4o or Claude) third, with uncertain or Critical-severity findings routed to human review. Outside SAST alert filtering, **no evidence was found** for published "cheap LLM first, escalate to frontier LLM" routing architectures or benchmarks evaluated on general pull-request code review (missing: precision/recall, latency, and token-cost curves for multi-model PR diff cascades) [inference].

### Risk-Based Code Review Standards and Inspection Economics

Formal inspection standards differentiate high-ceremony inspections from lightweight modern code review (MCR) by the severity of escaped defects [peer-reviewed, vendor or practitioner blog][^44]. Michael Fagan's IBM inspection process (Design and Code Inspections to Reduce Errors in Software Development, *IBM Systems Journal*, Vol. 15, pp. 182–211, 1976, https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1131845; extended in Advances in Software Inspections, *IEEE TSE*, 1986, https://research.ibm.com/publications/advances-in-software-inspections), standardized in IEEE Std 1028-1997 (IEEE, 1998, cited in https://www.wohlin.eu/stvr02-2.pdf, *Software Testing, Verification and Reliability*, Vol. 12, authors unnamed in retrieved text), defines an inspection as a visual examination to detect errors and specification deviations across six mandatory steps (Planning, Overview, Preparation, Meeting, Rework, Follow-up, plus optional causal analysis added in 1986) and four roles (author, moderator, reader, tester/reviewer) across 3–4 participants (or 3–7 per Wiegers) [peer-reviewed, vendor or practitioner blog][^45].  
Fagan inspections cap review speed to prevent defect-detection decay: ~500 non-commentary source statements (NCSS) per hour for overview, 100–125 NCSS/hour for individual preparation, and at most 125 NCSS/hour in meetings capped at 2 hours, with quantitative exit criteria estimated via defect seeding or capture-recapture such as Malotaux's limit of ≤0.25 remaining major defects per page (Fagan Inspection Process, SQRBOK, undated, https://sqrbok.github.io/content/verif/inspection/fagan-process.html, Sections 1 and 3) [vendor or practitioner blog][^46]. Later studies contest synchronous meetings because individual preparation uncovers nearly all defects (https://sqrbok.github.io/content/verif/inspection/fagan-process.html, "What is contested"; https://ianlotinsky.com/2010/07/13/best-kept-secrets-of-peer-code-review-by-jason-cohen/) [vendor or practitioner blog][^46]. Accordingly, SQRBOK recommends formal inspection only where missed defects are costly—requirements, architecture, safety- or security-critical code, and cross-team interfaces—and lightweight MCR for small code changes (https://sqrbok.github.io/content/verif/inspection/fagan-process.html) [vendor or practitioner blog][^46]. For lightweight MCR, secondary practitioner summaries of Jason Cohen's *Best Kept Secrets of Peer Code Review* (SmartBear Software, 2013 copyright, https://c2.etf.unsa.ba/file.php/121/pkks2015/best-kept-secrets-of-peer-code-review.pdf, Chapter 5, raw tables inaccessible) report across 2,500 Cisco reviews that defect detection peaks at 200–400 LOC (60–90 minutes at 2.22–6.66 LOC/min) and declines past 400 LOC (https://ianlotinsky.com/2010/07/13/best-kept-secrets-of-peer-code-review-by-jason-cohen/, July 13, 2010; https://www.ruzora.com/blog/the-right-size-for-a-code-review) [vendor or practitioner blog][^49]. A 2024 survey of MCR literature and practice (Modern Code Reviews - A Survey of Literature and Practice, Deepika Badampudi, Michael Unterkalmsteiner, and Ricardo Britto, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.00088) notes that MCR encompasses quality assurance, design improvement, knowledge transfer, ownership, and risk minimization, while observing across 571 papers evaluated by Microsoft practitioners (71% positive ratings) that academic citation count did not correlate with practitioner relevance [preprint][^44].

Two substantive disagreements in the literature directly affect merge-gate design. First, size and diffusion behave oppositely depending on evaluation objective and granularity: Kamei et al. (2013) found that while diffusion is risk-increasing when classifying whether a commit has any defect (RQ1), diffusion flips to risk-decreasing in commercial effort-aware ranking (RQ2) because spending a fixed line-inspection budget on smaller, concentrated changes yields more defects per inspected LOC (https://das.encs.concordia.ca/pdf/Kamei_TSE2013.pdf, RQ2–RQ3) [peer-reviewed][^55], echoing citation-summary evidence that smaller classes can be proportionally denser in defects per line than larger ones[^66] while larger modules hold more absolute defects (Koru et al., 2009, cited in[^54]). Second, vendor configuration examples often place \*.config.js in ignorePatterns (https://www.greptile.com/docs/code-review/controlling-nitpickiness) [vendor or practitioner blog][^7] or exclude \*\*/migrations/\*\* (https://www.codetoclarity.in/read/automating-code-reviews-coderabbit-complete-guide) [vendor or practitioner blog][^28], directly contradicting systems research documenting severe production outages from single-character configuration typos (Slater 2002, McNamara 2009 cited in[^12]) and systems failing to validate lazily read parameters at startup (Xu et al., OSDI 2016, https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi16/technical-sessions/presentation/xu) [peer-reviewed][^12].

## 3. Rules vs. Learned Risk Classifiers in a Local Setting

Published risk models rely on training pipelines and historical labels that a lightweight local gate cannot sustain [inference]. Classical JIT models (Kamei et al., 2013) train on 14 VCS metrics spanning 5 categories—including Diffusion (NS, ND, NF, Entropy), Size (LA, LD, LT), defect-fix status, and History/Experience metrics (AGE, NUC, REXP, and related developer experience features)—using retrospective SZZ labels linking bug-fixing commit messages to earlier defect-introducing commits (https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Summary-of-change-measures-Kamei-et-al-2013_tbl1_308911535; https://zhangyuqun.github.io/publications/issta2021.pdf, Section 3) [peer-reviewed][^2]. Deep neural JIT models (DeepJIT, CC2Vec) train CNN and hierarchical attention architectures on commit messages and diff token streams (https://zhangyuqun.github.io/publications/issta2021.pdf, Sections 1 and 4) [peer-reviewed][^2]. In security triage, compact fine-tuned transformers (CodeBERT, StarCoder 1B) require historical human accept/dismiss logs, with AquilaX recommending at least ~500 labeled examples per vulnerability class from the prior 6 months (https://aquilax.ai/blog/ai-sast-false-positive-triage, Steps 1 and 3) [vendor or practitioner blog][^11].  
Empirical evaluations show that learned JIT models transfer poorly across repositories and decay over time without retraining [peer-reviewed][^2]. When adapting Kamei's model to cross-project prediction (Studying just-in-time defect prediction using cross-project models, Yasutaka Kamei et al., undated in retrieved text, https://sailresearch.github.io/sail-website/data/pdfs/StudyingJustInTimeDefectPredictionUsingCross-projectModels.pdf), researchers had to drop 6 of the 14 features (the History and Experience categories) because project-specific histories do not exist on new projects or fail to generalize; cross-project performance only approached within-project accuracy when training projects were selectively clustered by domain-agnostic similarity [peer-reviewed][^54]. In Zeng et al.'s cross-project benchmark, DeepJIT's average AUC fell from 0.7065 (within-project) to 0.6808 (cross-project), and CC2Vec dropped on Eclipse Platform cross-project evaluation to 0.6574 AUC—underperforming both DBN-JIT (0.7113) and logistic regression LR-JIT (0.6946) (https://zhangyuqun.github.io/publications/issta2021.pdf, Section 4, Table 5) [peer-reviewed][^2].  
Even inside a single codebase, aging models suffer concept drift (Revisiting Just-In-Time Defect Prediction Models, Shane McIntosh and Yasutaka Kamei, ICSE 2018, https://www.computer.org/csdl/proceedings-article/icse/2018/563801a560/13l5NWNrH6N) [peer-reviewed][^70]. Comparing early (2011–2014) against late versions in Zeng et al. (Table 6), DeepJIT AUC dropped from 0.7144 to 0.6927 on QT and from 0.7491 to 0.6701 on Eclipse JDT; on Eclipse Platform, expanding training history from 10k to 50k commits pushed DeepJIT AUC from 0.7744 to 0.7769 and then *down* to 0.7610 (https://zhangyuqun.github.io/publications/issta2021.pdf, Section 4) [peer-reviewed][^2]. Grad-CAM interpretability further showed DeepJIT heavily weighting commit-message words (contributing to an 80.39% defect score) rather than diff logic (https://zhangyuqun.github.io/publications/issta2021.pdf, Section 3.2 / RQ1) [peer-reviewed][^2], while SZZ training labels remain noisy due to fix-authentication lag and tangled bug-fix commits (https://zhangyuqun.github.io/publications/issta2021.pdf, Section 3; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261022645_Non-essential_changes_in_version_histories) [peer-reviewed][^2]. Consequently, in a small local setting without a labeling and retraining pipeline, deterministic path/AST rules paired with a zero-training threshold heuristic on LA, NF, and Entropy beat static learned classifiers whenever labeled history is below ~500 items/class or project idioms evolve [inference]. Hand-written rules lose primarily when defect risk depends on semantic cross-file dataflows or novel vulnerability patterns where rule-based static analyzers suffer 60%–90% false-positive rates (https://aquilax.ai/blog/ai-sast-false-positive-triage; https://www.virelya.org/research/hybrid-sast-llm-code-security) [vendor or practitioner blog][^10]—precisely why rules should *gate* LLM review rather than replace it on executable code [inference].

## 4. The Inert Class and Asymmetric Misclassification Costs

No peer-reviewed evidence was found directly measuring whether LLM code review yields zero defect reduction on documentation, config, formatting, test-only, or rename PRs [inference]. Nevertheless, program-history analysis and commercial bot practice support a tightly bounded Inert tier [peer-reviewed, vendor or practitioner blog][^5]. Kawrykow and Robillard demonstrated across open-source repositories that up to 15.5% of method updates consist solely of non-essential, logic-preserving changes such as element renames (Non-essential changes in version histories, *ICSE '11*, May 2011, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261022645_Non-essential_changes_in_version_histories, Abstract) [peer-reviewed][^58]. Commercial tools detect inert or low-value targets through four layered checks: path-glob exclusions (path_filters in CodeRabbit, ignorePatterns in Greptile) covering lockfiles, binaries, generated artifacts, and markdown; PR metadata filters on bot authors (dependabot[bot]), labels, draft status, and branches; diff-hunk scoping (incremental_reviews, SonarQube "new code"); and syntactic/AST differencing such as Kawrykow and Robillard's non-essential detector, CodeRabbit's AST instructions, or CodeTracker (reported in a 2024 secondary summary as achieving 99.5% precision and recall within 3.6 seconds for block change history) [peer-reviewed, vendor or practitioner blog][^4].  
Two change categories commonly misclassified as inert—configuration files and test-only edits—must be handled conservatively [inference]. Parameter misconfigurations cause 70.0%–85.5% of configuration failures, 27%–51% of parameters interact with other parameters, and 21.7%–57.3% cross host or component boundaries (https://tianyin.github.io/pub/csur.pdf, Sections 2 and 2.4) [peer-reviewed][^12], meaning skipping \*.yaml, \*.json, or IaC changes risks production outages [inference]. Similarly, Markdown or YAML files that define AI agent instructions or gate policy (AGENTS.md, .cursorrules, .coderabbit.yaml, .greptile/config.json) alter downstream code generation or review rules (https://docs.coderabbit.ai/configuration/path-instructions; https://www.greptile.com/docs/code-review/greptile-json-reference) [vendor or practitioner blog][^5] and cannot be blindly ignored as \*.md documentation [inference]. Test-only changes sit in an intermediate zone: Snyk Code and AquilaX suppress or downweight static-analysis alerts inside test files (https://snyk.io/blog/snyk-code-priority-score-prioritizes-vulnerabilities/; https://aquilax.ai/blog/ai-sast-false-positive-triage) [vendor or practitioner blog][^11], and Greptile lists tests as an autoApprove candidate (https://www.greptile.com/docs/code-review/key-features) [vendor or practitioner blog][^30], yet test coverage fails to correlate with post-release bug safety (https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=4840\&context=sis_research) [peer-reviewed][^13], so PRs that delete or weaken test assertions require review [inference].  
Regarding asymmetric misclassification cost, **no evidence was found** measuring the economic, token, or defect-escape cost ratio between waiving a risky change as inert (false negative) and reviewing an inert change (false positive) in an LLM merge gate [inference]. Even Kamei et al.'s effort-aware evaluation notes as a limitation that it weights all defects equally rather than by severity (https://www.computer.org/csdl/journal/ts/2013/06/tts2013060757/13rRUxOvebA, Section 7) [peer-reviewed][^55]. The nearest operational benchmark comes from AquilaX's SAST triage targets, which tolerate a False Positive Rate under 20% while capping the False Negative Rate ("the safety metric") under 2%, noting that untuned enterprise SAST false-positive rates of 60%–90% destroy developer trust (https://aquilax.ai/blog/ai-sast-false-positive-triage, "Measuring Success") [vendor or practitioner blog][^11]. Datadog likewise observes a direct tension between filtering noise and missing real bugs (https://www.datadoghq.com/blog/using-llms-to-filter-out-false-positives/) [vendor or practitioner blog][^42]. In a pre-merge LLM gate, wasting a single LLM pass on an inert diff costs trivial tokens and latency (and zero developer friction if clean passes suppress inline comments, like Greptile's statusCheck: true), whereas falsely exempting a broken logic, config, or security change risks production incidents (https://www.greptile.com/docs/code-review/greptile-json-reference; https://tianyin.github.io/pub/csur.pdf) [peer-reviewed, vendor or practitioner blog][^12] [inference].

## 5. Concrete Local Merge Gate Specification

Using only git diff, --numstat, changed paths, and optional branch task descriptions (plus optional read-only local git log and local deterministic linters/SAST), your gate should extract zero-training structural signals and evaluate classes top-down (Elevated overrides first, Inert only when strictly verified across all touched files, Standard as the default) [inference].

### Features and Local Transferability

| Feature | Local Computation | Grounding Source | Transferability to Local Git Gate |

| Modified files (NF), directories (ND), & Entropy | Count changed files (NF), unique parent dirs (ND), and Shannon entropy of per-file (LA + LD) shares | Kamei et al. 2013 (NF, ND, Entropy in OSS RQ1–RQ3[^54]) | **High (Direct)** — computable directly from --numstat; recall commercial RQ2 effort-weighting reversal[^55] [inference] |
| Structural non-essentialness (is_non_essential) | Check git diff -M for 100% pure renames (0 changed lines), lockfile/generated globs, and LA_sem == 0 after comment/whitespace stripping (or lightweight AST diff) | Kawrykow & Robillard 2011 (15.5% non-essential[^58]); CodeRabbit/Greptile globs[^5] | **High (Direct)** — deterministic diff/AST normalization resists tangled-commit bypass[^58] [inference] |
| Path sensitivity (is_config, is_critical, is_gate_config, is_test_only) | Repo-local glob table matching configs (\*.yaml, \*.json, \*.toml, .env\*, IaC), critical logic (auth, crypto, db, migrations, api), gate/agent files (AGENTS.md, .coderabbit.yaml, .greptile), and test paths | Configuration survey (70.0%–85.5% param errors[^12]); CodeRabbit/Greptile path scoping[^4]; Snyk test weighting[^38] | **Medium-High** — directory layouts vary across repos, requiring a small per-repo glob map pinned on the base branch[^22] [inference] |
| Task intent (is_fix_or_revert, is_ai_origin) | Match branch task description for fix/bug/hotfix/revert/security keywords and agent/bot branch metadata | Kamei et al. 2013 (FIX risk-increasing[^55]); CodeRabbit/Moderne AI caveats[^21] | **Medium** — transfers only where branch descriptions exist and follow consistent naming [inference] |
| *Optional local git log*: File AGE & change bursts | git log -n 5 --format=%ct -- \<files> for time since last commit and recent commit bursts | Kamei et al. 2013 (AGE risk-decreasing[^55]); Microsoft change bursts[^19] | **High in full local clones**; **None** in --depth=1 shallow clones [inference] |
| *Optional local static proxy*: Caller references & local SAST | Regex/AST-grep reference counts for changed symbols + local Semgrep/linter pass | Zimmermann & Nagappan 2008 (dependency centrality[^18]); SAST-Genius[^10] | **Medium** — local grep/AST-grep only approximates binary dependency graphs[^61] [inference] |

### Three-Class Routing Matrix and Treatments

| Class | Classification Rule (Evaluated Top-Down) | Merge-Gate Treatment | Empirical Basis & Transferability Caveat |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Elevated | Triggers if **any** holds: LA_sem > 400 (or > 200 on critical paths — uncalibrated [inference] thresholds anchored to Cisco human saturation); high file/subsystem diffusion (NF & Entropy outliers); touches is_critical, is_gate_config, or multi-component is_config; is_fix_or_revert modifying executable code; deleted/weakened test assertions; optional short AGE / burst; or any Critical/High local SAST / test-failure burst [inference] | **One elevated frontier LLM review with expanded context + orthogonal second check**: Pass diff plus caller/schema/config context files (Greptile/SAST-Genius pattern) at strict logic/security focus (strictness: 1 / commentTypes: ["logic"]), run upstream SAST so LLM validates rather than lints, and require human approval (recommending PR split if LA_sem > 400) [inference] | SmartBear/Cisco 200–400 LOC decay[^49]; Kamei NF/fix/AGE[^55]; LApredict[^2]; Yin et al. config coupling[^12]; Zimmermann & Nagappan centrality[^18]; SAST-Genius[^10]; Fagan critical-code inspection[^46]. Prefer orthogonal human/SAST review over running two *identical* LLMs, since arXiv:2601.02941v1 finds models share correlated blind spots ("all correct or all wrong together")[^72] [preprint] [inference] |
| Inert | Triggers only if **zero** Elevated flags fire **and every** touched file satisfies one of: lockfile/generated build glob; pure prose docs (\*.md, \*.txt, excluding is_gate_config); pure whitespace/comment edits (LA_sem == 0 under -w + comment strip); or 100% pure rename (git diff -M, 0 edited lines); **and** local formatters/secret-scans pass [inference] | **No LLM review (0 tokens)**: Auto-pass the LLM gate once deterministic format, lockfile-integrity, and secret scanners succeed [inference] | Kawrykow & Robillard 15.5% non-essential updates[^58]; CodeRabbit path_filters[^5]; Greptile ignorePatterns/autoApprove[^7]; AquilaX Tier-1 rule suppression[^11]. Note: skipping pure doc/rename diffs rests on structural inference rather than measured LLM defect-yield trials [inference] |
| Standard | Default fallback: bounded logic edits, test additions (with intact assertions), or isolated single-file config tweaks with LA_sem ≤ 400, low diffusion, no critical/gate paths, and clean local SAST [inference] | **One diff-scoped frontier/standard LLM review**: Single balanced pass (chill / strictness: 2, logic + syntax only, suppressing style nits), scoped to changed hunks plus immediate enclosing functions [inference] | Google 1-reviewer sufficiency[^16]; SonarQube "Clean as You Code" new-code focus[^9]; Greptile strictness: 27; CodeRabbit chill[^4] |

### Misclassification Hedges and Escalation Controls

To keep false-negative risk aligned with AquilaX's <2% FNR safety target (https://aquilax.ai/blog/ai-sast-false-positive-triage) [vendor or practitioner blog][^11] without forfeiting token savings, the gate should enforce five operational hedges [inference]:

> 1. **Pin policy config to the base branch**: Load all Inert path globs, critical-path lists, and size thresholds strictly from the target/base branch (or outside the repository), never from the PR's source branch—mirroring Greptile's autoApprove safeguard so a branch cannot exempt itself (https://www.greptile.com/docs/code-review/greptile-json-reference) [vendor or practitioner blog][^22].  
> 2. **Escalate mixed and tangled diffs at the PR level**: Require Inert conditions to hold across *every* modified file; if a PR mixes documentation/renames with even one executable or config hunk, route the executable/config files to Standard or Elevated rather than letting tangled edits slip through (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261022645_Non-essential_changes_in_version_histories) [peer-reviewed][^58] [inference].  
> 3. **Force-promote on deterministic SAST or test-failure bursts**: Always run fast local SAST, secret scanners, and unit tests upstream; any Critical/High SAST hit or consecutive test-failure burst overrides Inert/Standard into Elevated (https://aquilax.ai/blog/ai-sast-false-positive-triage; https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/Using-Pre-Release-Test-Failures-to-Build-Early-Post-Release-Defect-Prediction-Models.pdf) [peer-reviewed, vendor or practitioner blog][^11] [inference].  
> 4. **Fail closed and escalate on review failure**: When a Standard or Elevated LLM call times out, errors out, hits rate limits, or flags low confidence/unparsed output, fail the merge gate and escalate to human review—adopting CodeRabbit's fail_commit_status: true posture (https://docs.coderabbit.ai/reference/configuration) and AquilaX's drift/uncertainty escalation (https://aquilax.ai/blog/ai-sast-false-positive-triage) [vendor or practitioner blog][^4] [inference].  
> 5. **Audit-sample the Inert tier and track escapes**: Force-route a fixed random audit sample (an uncalibrated [inference] starting band such as 5%–10% of Inert PRs, emphasizing boundary cases like test-only edits, doc PRs, and large renames) to a Standard review, and feed any post-merge hotfixes or reverts on Inert-cleared commits back into stricter path exclusions (https://aquilax.ai/blog/ai-sast-false-positive-triage) [vendor or practitioner blog][^11] [inference].

## Claims Considered Least Certain

> 1. AI-generated PRs introduce 1.7× more defects (https://www.coderabbit.ai/whitepapers/state-of-AI-vs-human-code-generation-report) [vendor or practitioner blog][^67]: Sourced from a gated vendor marketing whitepaper landing page without exposed sampling, statistical controls, confidence intervals, or peer review.  
> 2. SAST-Genius achieving 100% recall and 89.5% precision (https://www.virelya.org/research/hybrid-sast-llm-code-security) [vendor or practitioner blog][^10]: Evaluated on an unverified 170-vulnerability benchmark published on a vendor/research site without peer review or data-leakage auditing.  
> 3. CLEVER achieving 79% precision and 65% recall (https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-large-scale-empirical-study-of-just-in-time-Kamei-Shihab/04eb410c0a59fc08a4648af43f7de323cc7d06c2)[^66]: Appears solely inside a secondary citation summary without accessible authors, venue details, project count, or evaluation methodology.  
> 4. AquilaX's 60%–90% untuned SAST FP rate, <2% FNR target, and "below 85% confidence" rule (https://aquilax.ai/blog/ai-sast-false-positive-triage) [vendor or practitioner blog][^11]: Practitioner targets rather than controlled empirical measurements; additionally, its sentence "Suppress findings below 85% confidence in the false positive class only" conflicts with "Everything in between goes to manual review" and appears to be an editorial inversion of >85%.  
> 5. SmartBear/Cisco 200–400 LOC saturation curve across 2,500 reviews (https://ianlotinsky.com/2010/07/13/best-kept-secrets-of-peer-code-review-by-jason-cohen/, https://www.ruzora.com/blog/the-right-size-for-a-code-review) [vendor or practitioner blog][^49]: Drawn from secondary practitioner summaries of an industry vendor book without inspectable raw tables or regression controls, and human cognitive fatigue past 400 LOC does not automatically calibrate LLM context-window degradation [inference].  
> 6. **Zero-yield assumption for Inert PR classes and exact asymmetric cost ratios [inference]**: No accessible empirical study quantifies LLM code-review defect yield across documentation, test-only, reformat, or deletion PRs, nor measures the exact cost ratio between missed risky PRs and wasted tokens on inert PRs.  
> 7. Transferability of Windows binary dependency networks (Zimmermann & Nagappan, 200818) and multi-year OSS SZZ predictors (Kamei et al., 201353) to uncalibrated local git diff thresholds [inference]: Approximating compiled dependency centrality with local text/AST-grep, and setting numeric cutoffs such as LA_sem > 400 or 5%–10% audit sampling without local project calibration, rely on engineering inference rather than direct local-gate benchmarks.
